Cavitation: boiling water inside a cold pump

A pump pulls its inlet pressure down. If the pressure anywhere inside falls to the water's vapor pressure, the water boils, and the bubbles then implode against the impeller. NPSH is how we check that this will not happen.

Two numbers, one question

Both quantities are heads in metres of the liquid being pumped. They answer one question: does the water arrive with more pressure above boiling than the pump will take away?

NPSHr is not the point where cavitation starts

Vendors test by holding flow constant and lowering suction pressure until head falls 3%. That value is the NPSH3 printed on the curve as NPSHr. Enough vapor to cost 3% of head means bubbles have been forming for some time.

First bubbles appear at the higher incipient value, NPSHi, often 2 to 5 times NPSH3 at design flow, and more on high-energy pumps. This is why Hydraulic Institute guidance asks for an NPSHa/NPSHr ratio above 1, commonly about 1.1 to 2.5 depending on suction energy and service.

Why a collapsing bubble hurts

  1. GrowsLocal pressure is below vapor pressure, so liquid flashes to vapor.
  2. TravelsThe flow carries the bubble toward the vane tips, where pressure is higher.
  3. CollapsesVapor condenses at once. The side away from the wall caves in first and forms a jet.
  4. StrikesThe jet and shock wave hit the metal. Thousands of hits per second fatigue the surface into pits.
Collapse time, Rayleigh estimate
t ≈ 0.915 R √(ρ/Δp)
R = 1 mm, Δp = 300 kPa
t ≈ 0.915 × 10⁻³ × √(1000 / 300000) ≈ 53 µs
Jet impact, water-hammer estimate
p ≈ ρ c v
v ≈ 100 m/s (order of magnitude), c ≈ 1480 m/s
p ≈ 1000 × 1480 × 100 ≈ 150 MPa

150 MPa is the same order as the strength of cast iron and bronze impellers, delivered to a spot microns across, again and again.

Worked check

Same pump and pipe as the rig above. Water at 60 °C from an open tank with its surface 3 m below the pump, at Q = 100 m³/h.

pv = 19.9 kPa, ρ = 983 kg/m³, ρg = 9645 N/m³
(patm − pv)/ρg = (101.3 − 19.9) × 1000 / 9645 = 8.44 m
NPSHa = 8.44 − 3.00 − hf(1.20) = 4.24 m
NPSHr at 100 m³/h = 4.50 m   ratio = 0.94
Not acceptable: the pump is past its 3% head-loss point.

Lowering the pump 2 m gives NPSHa = 6.24 m and a ratio of 1.39. Note that this water would be fine at 25 °C: the same suction arrangement gives NPSHa = 5.84 m, a ratio of 1.30.

How to stop it

Raise NPSHa

  • Raise the tank level or lower the pump
  • Use a larger, shorter suction line with fewer fittings
  • Clean strainers and keep suction valves fully open
  • Cool the liquid, or pressurize the tank

Lower NPSHr

  • Reduce flow by throttling the discharge, never the suction
  • Slow the pump with a drive: NPSHr falls roughly with N²
  • Pick a pump with a larger eye or double suction
  • Add an inducer ahead of the impeller
