Module 1

How pumps work

Every pump adds energy to a liquid. Pumps are grouped by how that energy gets in: positive displacement pumps push pockets of liquid, while dynamic pumps use a spinning impeller. This course follows the centrifugal pump.

Study in this order
  • AnimationCh. 1 Families, Ch. 2 Impellers, Ch. 3 Housings, Ch. 4 Inside the pump
  • Slidesp. 3 learning objectives · p. 4 pump types · p. 5 operation of a dynamic pump
Key ideas
  • Positive displacement: reciprocating (pistons, plungers, diaphragms, bellows) or rotary (vanes, screws, lobes).
  • Dynamic: centrifugal sends liquid out radially, propeller sends it axially, mixed flow does both.
  • Inside a centrifugal pump: liquid enters at the eye, the impeller throws it outward and raises its kinetic energy, and the widening volute slows it down so kinetic energy turns into pressure.
  • Net result: a large pressure rise, with kinetic energy at the outlet about where it started (ΔKE ≈ 0).
Check yourself
What job does the volute do?
It is a passage that keeps widening. The liquid slows down there, and its kinetic energy is converted into pressure. (Animation Ch. 4; slide p. 5)
Comparing the inlet and outlet of a centrifugal pump, which form of energy changes the most?
Pressure. Kinetic energy ends up about where it started, so the net result is a large rise in pressure, ΔP. (Animation Ch. 4)
A propeller pump and a centrifugal pump both use an impeller. What is the difference?
The direction the liquid leaves. Centrifugal sends it radially, away from the shaft. Propeller pushes it axially, along the shaft. (Animation Ch. 2; slide p. 4)
Module 2

Measuring performance and reading pump curves

A pump's performance is measured on a test bench and published by the manufacturer as a pump curve. Learning to read that chart is the core skill for everything that follows.

Study in this order
  • AnimationCh. 5 Test bench, Ch. 6 Data reduction, Ch. 7 The chart, Ch. 8 Booklets
  • Slidesp. 6–7 measurements and equations · p. 10–11 reading a pump chart
  • AnimationCh. 9 Reading, Ch. 10 Which power?
  • Slidesp. 12–14 the same worked example on the slides
  • AnimationCh. 11 Extremes, Ch. 12 Best efficiency
  • Slidesp. 8–9 cutoff head and free delivery
  • Try itCh. 13 Try it: drag on the chart and read capacity, head, efficiency and power
  • PracticeSlide p. 15 example pump problem (answers given on the slide) · then Example 1
Key equations
Hp = Hd − Hs
Hp = (Pd/γ + Vd²/2g + zd) − (Ps/γ + Vs²/2g + zs)
Ẇ = ṁ g Hp   power input to the liquid
Ẇbhp = T ω   power input to the impeller (brake horsepower)
ηp = Ẇ / Ẇbhp
Slide p. 7, Eqs. (1)–(5)
Key ideas
  • A pump chart is for one pump model at one speed. It shows a head curve for each impeller diameter, iso-efficiency lines, iso-power lines, and the NPSH required curve.
  • The power read from the chart is the brake horsepower, the power into the impeller, not the power into the liquid.
  • Cutoff head (point a): no flow, highest head. Free delivery (point b): no head, highest flow. Efficiency is zero at both ends, with a maximum between them: the best efficiency point (bep).
Check yourself
The chart gives 10 hp at the operating point. Is that the power into the liquid or into the impeller?
Into the impeller (brake horsepower). For the worked example, the power into the liquid is ṁgHp = 7.08 hp, which is less than 10 hp. Their ratio, 7.08/10 = 0.708, matches the ~71% read from the chart. (Slide p. 14; Animation Ch. 10)
A 6½-inch impeller produces 160 ft of head. What are the flow, power and efficiency?
175 gpm, 10 hp, and about 71% efficiency. (Slides p. 12–13; Animation Ch. 9)
Why is efficiency zero at both cutoff head and free delivery?
Because Ẇ = ṁgHp is zero with no flow, and zero with no head. (Animation Ch. 11; slides p. 8–9)
Module 3

The system curve and pump selection

A pump must deliver the right flow while overcoming the elevation and friction of the pipe system. The system curve describes what the pipes need; the pump curve describes what the pump can give. Where they cross is where the system actually runs.

Study in this order
  • Slidesp. 16–17 what the system curve is
  • Slidesp. 18–26 worked example: acetone system, select a pump for 300 gpm
  • Slidesp. 27–28 general observations for selection
  • AnimationRevisit Ch. 12 Best efficiency
  • PracticeExample 2: select a Bell & Gossett e-1531 pump
Key equation: energy balance between two points
Hp = (z2 − z1) + (f L/D + ΣK) V²/2g
Slide p. 19 (tank-to-tank system, with P and V at the free surfaces cancelling). Example 2 writes the same thing in terms of flow rate: Hp = (z2 − z1) + (f L/D + ΣK) 8V̇²/(π²gD⁴)
Key ideas (slides p. 27–28)
  • You must find the system curve before you can size a pump.
  • The operating point is where the pump curve and the system curve intersect.
  • If the pump's ports differ from the pipe size, the reducer and expander add resistance. Recalculate the system curve.
  • Added resistance, such as closing a valve, shifts the system curve to the left. That changes the operating point, the efficiency and the bhp.
  • Select pumps to run slightly to the right of the maximum efficiency. Pumps selected to the left may fail their motors if unforeseen resistance drops efficiency too far.
Check yourself
What does partially closing a valve do to the system curve?
It adds resistance, which shifts the system curve to the left. Head rises and capacity falls. (Slides p. 26–28)
Why choose an operating point slightly to the right of the bep rather than to the left?
Unforeseen resistances move the operating point left: head rises, capacity drops, and efficiency increases. Starting on the left risks efficiency dropping too much, which may cause motor failure. (Slide p. 28)
In the slide example, which pump was selected and where does it operate?
Bell & Gossett Series 1531, Model 3BC at 1150 rpm with the 8-inch impeller: 310 gpm at 24 ft, efficiency 77.4%, 2.4 hp brake horsepower. (Slides p. 23 and 26)
Module 4

Cavitation and NPSH

A pump pulls its inlet pressure down. If the pressure anywhere inside drops to the liquid's vapor pressure, the liquid boils even when it is cold, and the bubbles then collapse against the impeller. Net Positive Suction Head (NPSH) is how you check that this will not happen.

Study in this order
  • AnimationCavitation and NPSH: let all nine steps play (Healthy → The fix), then scroll down to the notes and the single-bubble close-up
  • Slidesp. 29 cavitation · p. 30–32 NPSHa, NPSHr, flooded suction vs suction lift
  • Slidesp. 33–38 worked example: where to place the pump to prevent cavitation
  • Try itBack in the cavitation animation, move the sliders: raise the temperature, clog the strainer, lower the tank, and watch NPSHa and the margin ratio
  • PracticeExample 3: maximum pump height, and the effect of water temperature
Key equations
NPSHa = Ps/γ − Pv/γ
NPSHa = P1/γ + (z1 − zs) − (f L/D + ΣK + 1) Vs²/2g − Pv/γ
Cavitation is prevented when NPSHa > NPSHr
Slides p. 31–32, Eqs. (9)–(10). (z1 − zs) is negative for suction lift and positive for flooded suction.
Key ideas
  • NPSHa (available) belongs to the system: tank, pipe and liquid. You calculate it. It falls as flow rises.
  • NPSHr (required) belongs to the pump. You read it from the manufacturer's curve. It rises as flow rises.
  • NPSHa > 0 is not enough: pressure keeps dropping just inside the pump, before the impeller.
  • Suction lift systems are more prone to cavitation than flooded suction.
  • Hot liquid has a higher vapor pressure, so NPSHa drops. In Example 3, above 76 °C the pump must sit below the water surface.
  • Collapsing bubbles send pressure waves into the metal, causing pitting (cavitation erosion), noise, vibration and lost head.
Heads-up: which safety margin?

The lecture slides recommend NPSHa − NPSHr ≥ 3 ft (slide p. 32), and the slide example uses that 3 ft. The Example 3 solution uses a safety factor of 1 m. The cavitation animation also explains that the NPSHr printed on a curve is the point where head has already dropped 3%, and mentions Hydraulic Institute ratios of NPSHa/NPSHr. If you are not sure which margin to use on an assignment, use the one the problem gives, or ask your instructor.

Check yourself
If NPSHa is positive, is the pump safe from cavitation?
Not necessarily. NPSHa ≥ 0 means no boiling at the inlet, but cavitation may still occur because of the pressure drop before the impeller. You need NPSHa > NPSHr. (Slides p. 31–32)
A flow-control valve has to go somewhere. Suction line or discharge line?
Discharge line. Valves in the suction line can cause cavitation. (Slide p. 54; the animation's "How to stop it" list says the same)
In the animation, why does hot water cavitate at a flow that was safe with cool water?
Vapor pressure rises with temperature, so the whole NPSHa line drops. NPSHr stays the same because the pump has not changed. (Animation step 7 "Hot water")
In the slide example (water at 100 °F, Model 2AC at 3500 rpm), how high above the tank surface can the pump go?
h = 9.571 ft. This is the maximum: any higher may cause cavitation from the extra suction head loss. (Slides p. 37–38)
Module 5

Affinity laws

Dimensional analysis shows how a pump's flow, head and power scale when you change its speed or impeller diameter. That lets you estimate performance at conditions not printed on the chart.

Study in this order
  • Slidesp. 39–40 dimensional analysis and similitude
  • Slidesp. 41–46 worked example: 1750 rpm to 3500 rpm
  • Slidesp. 47 affinity laws for speed changes
  • PracticeExample 4 (new speed and diameter) and Example 5 (refit with a variable speed drive)
Key equations (constant impeller diameter)
V̇n/V̇o = ωn/ωo
Hp,n/Hp,o = (ωn/ωo)²
Ẇn/Ẇo = (ωn/ωo)³
ηn = ηo
Slide p. 47, Eqs. (20)–(23). When diameter also changes, equate V̇/(ωD³), gHp/(ω²D²) and Ẇ/(ρω³D⁵), as in the Example 4 solution.
Key ideas
  • The laws assume efficiency, head coefficient and power coefficient depend only weakly on the rotational Reynolds number.
  • They are estimates. In the slide example they predicted 10 hp and 64% at 3500 rpm, while the manufacturer's curve gave 9.5 hp and 67.5%. Close, but not exact.
  • The affinity laws scale pump performance. They do not tell you the new operating point of the pump/pipe system. For that you still need the system curve (Example 5, part d).
Check yourself
A pump gives 80 gpm, 40 ft and 1.25 hp at 1750 rpm. Estimate its performance at 3500 rpm.
Speed doubles, so flow × 2 = 160 gpm, head × 4 = 160 ft, power × 8 = 10 hp, efficiency unchanged at 64%. (Slides p. 42–46)
Why did the affinity-law point and the new operating point in Example 5 not match?
The point from part (b) lies on the scaled pump curve, but the operating point in part (c) is where the new pump curve meets the system curve. The affinity laws scale pump performance curves; they do not scale pump/pipe system operating points. (Example 5 solution, part d)
Module 6

Design practice

Practical guidelines that tie the earlier modules together when you lay out a real pump and pipe system.

Study
  • Slidesp. 48–55 design practices
Guidelines from the slides
  • Economics: lay out the network wisely and size pipes close to the economic diameter. A slightly larger pipe can allow for future flow increases. (p. 49–50)
  • Noise and vibration: keep pressure drop to about 25–30 psi per 1000 ft for liquids. (p. 52)
  • Pump placement: place the pump to avoid cavitation, with an NPSHa analysis, especially for suction lift. (p. 53)
  • Flow control: single-speed motors need valves (usually globe valves). Variable speed motors cost more, and the affinity laws estimate their effect. (p. 53)
  • Valve placement: avoid valves for flow control if possible. If you need one, put it on the discharge line. (p. 54)
  • Entrained gas: give the pipe a slight rise in the flow direction and remove gas with bleed ports or expansion tanks. (p. 55)

Reference

Key terms

Pump head, Hp
Energy the pump adds to the liquid, expressed as a height of that liquid: discharge head minus suction head.Module 2 · slide p. 7
Brake horsepower, Ẇbhp
Shaft power into the impeller, T ω. This is the power printed on pump curves.Module 2 · slides p. 7, 14
Pump efficiency, ηp
Power delivered to the liquid divided by brake horsepower.Module 2 · slide p. 7
Iso-efficiency and iso-power lines
Lines on a pump chart joining points of equal efficiency and equal input power across the impeller curves.Module 2 · Animation Ch. 7
Cutoff head
The no-flow end of a pump curve: highest head, zero flow.Module 2 · slide p. 8
Free delivery
The no-head end of a pump curve: the liquid passes through as if the pump were a pipe.Module 2 · slide p. 9
Best efficiency point (bep)
The flow at which an impeller curve reaches its maximum efficiency.Modules 2–3 · Animation Ch. 11–12
System curve
System head as a function of flow rate: the pressure, velocity and elevation heads plus losses the pump must overcome.Module 3 · slide p. 16
Operating point
Where the pump curve and the system curve intersect.Module 3 · slide p. 27
Cavitation
Liquid boils where local pressure falls to vapor pressure; the bubbles collapse in higher pressure and erode the impeller.Module 4 · slide p. 29 · Animation 2
NPSH available, NPSHa
Head at the pump suction above the vapor pressure head. Set by the system; you calculate it.Module 4 · slide p. 31
NPSH required, NPSHr
Suction head the pump needs, determined by the manufacturer and shown on the pump curve.Module 4 · slide p. 32
Suction lift vs flooded suction
Liquid surface below the pump (lift) or above it (flooded). Lift systems are more prone to cavitation.Module 4 · slides p. 30–31
Affinity laws
Scaling rules for flow, head and power when pump speed or impeller diameter changes.Module 5 · slide p. 47
